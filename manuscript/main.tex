\begin{abstract}
Temporal memory changes interface roughening, but comparing numerical results
requires specifying both the driving process and the observation protocol.
We examine a shared-innovation autoregressive (AR) sum and two Gaussian
spectral filters in parallel-update ballistic deposition. Validation of the
final binary space--time field detects an axis-assignment fault that retains
correct one-dimensional sequences. Exact AR covariances retain cross terms
between modes driven by common innovations. Matched campaigns with 64
independent runs per cell and $L=32$--$512$ show increasing finite-size
late-time width slopes for all three inputs. At $\theta=0.25$, the slopes are
$\AlphaSharedQuarter$ for shared AR and $\AlphaFgnQuarter$ for the
fGn-spectrum filter. A paired bootstrap does not resolve a slope difference
between the Gaussian filters at any of the seven tested parameters.
Additional controls compare nested observation windows under one fixed
spectral record, separating duration sensitivity from changes in its cutoff.
The nearest-neighbour increment distribution reduces exactly to the flat-pair
fraction, so its Hellinger distance describes local probability changes rather
than independent spatial information. These results provide a controlled
finite-window comparison and specify the input and inference checks needed
before drawing asymptotic scaling conclusions.
\end{abstract}

Kardar--Parisi--Zhang, finite-size scaling, stochastic simulation}

\section{Introduction}
The Kardar--Parisi--Zhang (KPZ) equation describes the competition between
smoothing, lateral growth and stochastic driving in a fluctuating interface
\cite{Kardar1986,BarabasiStanley1995},
\begin{equation}
 \partial_t h=\nu\nabla^2h+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\nabla h)^2+\eta.
 \label{eq:kpz}
\end{equation}
For short-range noise in one dimension, the roughness and dynamic exponents
are $\alpha=1/2$ and $z=3/2$. Their stability when the noise retains temporal
memory remains a problem in nonequilibrium statistical physics.

A conventional continuum description introduces the low-frequency spectrum
\cite{Medina1989,SquizzatoCanet2019}
\begin{equation}
 D(\omega,k)=D_0+D_\theta|\omega|^{-2\theta},\qquad0<\theta<1/2,
 \label{eq:noise}
\end{equation}
with short-range spatial correlations and a long-time covariance contribution
proportional to $|t-t'|^{2\theta-1}$. The exponent, relative amplitudes and
short-time regularisation are distinct properties. A discrete fGn covariance
specifies more than this long-time power. Matching its complete shape does not
by itself identify a continuum universality class.

Thresholds in this literature also concern different physical questions.
The nonperturbative renormalisation-group treatment finds a short-range
stability boundary at $\theta=1/6$ in one dimension \cite{SquizzatoCanet2019}.
Al\'es and L\'opez identify a boundary near $0.25$ from the separation of
global and spectral roughness exponents and the appearance of anomalous,
faceted growth \cite{AlesLopez2019}. Height-distribution markers and emergence
diagnostics probe other observables \cite{JiangXia2026,WangXia2025}.
These definitions are not interchangeable estimates of one critical parameter.
Model and discretisation dependence under temporally correlated driving has
also been emphasised \cite{RodriguezFernandez2024}.

A numerical comparison must establish which random field reaches the growth
rule. An array assembly can retain correctly filtered sequences while changing
whether their correlations act in space or time. A second issue arises in
inference: a finite-ensemble distance has a nonzero null floor, and its first
detectable deviation depends on sampling and system size. Neither issue is
resolved by fitting one exponent to an entire finite-lag covariance curve.

The question addressed here is which finite-window comparisons remain
interpretable when the complete input process and its implementation are
specified. We validate the assembled noise field, retain the cross terms in
the shared-innovation AR covariance, and compare three inputs on a matched
finite-size grid. A paired analysis of the Gaussian filters uses their common
random streams rather than treating their agreement as independent replication.
Additional duration controls keep the spectral record fixed while extending
the observation window. Together these checks separate implementation validity,
differences between specified processes, and sensitivity to observation time.

Hellinger distance and novelty detection have already been used in this
setting \cite{WangXia2025}, and microscopic sensitivity has been discussed
\cite{RodriguezFernandez2024}. Our contribution is the quantitative comparison
under these explicit controls, rather than a new distance metric or filtering
method. The exact nearest-neighbour increment probability mass function (pmf)
also shows which morphology information a local distance retains. Our
conclusions concern the tested finite windows, not an inferred critical point
or a reconstruction of historical implementations.

\section{Model and noise processes}
\label{sec:model}
\subsection{Parallel-update ballistic deposition}
We use a periodic lattice, a flat initial condition and the parallel update
\begin{equation}
 h(x,t+1)=\max\{h(x,t)+\ders(x,t),h(x-1,t),h(x+1,t)\},
 \label{eq:bd}
\end{equation}
where $\ders=\mathbf1[\eta_\theta>0]$ is binary deposition. This model belongs
to the family studied with temporally correlated driving
\cite{Lam1992,AlesLopez2019,JiangXia2026}. Latent sequences are independent
between sites. The spectral inputs are Gaussian; the uniform-driven AR input
below is not assumed to be Gaussian.

Equation~\eqref{eq:bd} preserves $|h(x+1,t)-h(x,t)|\le1$ from a flat state.
Each site rises by zero or one per sweep, but can rise because of a higher
neighbour even when its own deposition variable is zero. The rule does not
reduce to independent deposition (Appendix~\ref{app:single}). Periodicity
gives $\sum_x\Delta h=0$, so up- and down-step counts are equal in every
configuration. With $p_{\mathrm{flat}}=P(\Delta h=0)$, the increment pmf is
\begin{equation}
 P(\Delta h)=\left(\frac{1-p_{\mathrm{flat}}}{2},p_{\mathrm{flat}},
                   \frac{1-p_{\mathrm{flat}}}{2}\right).
 \label{eq:pmf}
\end{equation}
This is a local descriptor, not an assumed order parameter. Different spatial
arrangements of the same steps can produce different large-scale interfaces.

\subsection{Shared-innovation AR sum}
The streaming construction examined here uses geometrically spaced modes,
\begin{align}
 u_n&=aB^{-n},\quad r_n=e^{-u_n},\quad(a,B)=(6,2),\\
 X_n(t)&=r_nX_n(t-1)+z(t),\qquad\eta_\theta(t)=\sum_{n=1}^NW_nX_n(t),\\
 W_n^2&=(1-r_n^2)K_\theta u_n^{1-2\theta},\quad
 K_\theta=\frac{B^{1/2-\theta}-B^{\theta-1/2}}{\Gamma(2-2\theta)}.
 \label{eq:ar}
\end{align}
Centred innovations $z(t)\sim U[-1/2,1/2]$ are independent across time and
sites but shared by all modes at a given site and time. Modes start at zero,
with $N=\max[16,\lceil\log_B(aT)\rceil+4]$. A common weight rescaling changes
raw variance but not the thresholded sequence. At exactly $\theta=0$ the
simulator uses a separate i.i.d. binary reference. The unbranched AR formula
remains correlated there.

We call this the \emph{shared AR} variant. Its weights are related to the
fast fractional Gaussian noise family \cite{Mandelbrot1971}, but a family name
or a symbol $z_t(u_n)$ does not establish innovation covariance across modes.
We do not assume every cited simulation uses these streams, initialisation or
white-noise shortcut.

Writing $Q_{nm}=\langle z_n(t)z_m(t)\rangle$, the exact stationary finite-mode
covariance is
\begin{equation}
 C_N(\tau)=\sum_{n,m}W_nW_mr_n^\tau\frac{Q_{nm}}{1-r_nr_m},\quad\tau\ge0.
 \label{eq:arcov}
\end{equation}
Shared innovations have $Q_{nm}=1/12$ for all pairs; independent innovations
have $Q_{nm}=\delta_{nm}/12$, reducing Eq.~\eqref{eq:arcov} to a diagonal sum.
These define different processes. Both are compared analytically, while the
production AR campaign uses the shared case only. Appendix~\ref{app:cov}
addresses cold starts and the interpretation of a power-law approximation.

\subsection{Gaussian spectral filters}
For each run and site we filter a length-$T$ Gaussian white sequence in Fourier
space, then threshold it. The power-law input uses
\begin{equation}
 S_{\mathrm{power}}(f)=|f|^{-2\theta},\qquad f\ne0.
 \label{eq:power}
\end{equation}
The second input uses the fGn spectral shape,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{gathered}
 S_{\mathrm{fGn}}(f)\propto(1-\cos2\pi f)
 \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}|2\pi(f+k)|^{-(2H+1)},\\
 H=\theta+\tfrac12.
 \end{gathered}
 \label{eq:fgnspectrum}
\end{equation}
The alias sum is evaluated for $k=-40,\ldots,40$. Both spectra are sampled on
a finite-record Fourier grid and have DC removed. They are finite-period
approximations with a particular low-frequency cutoff, not exact infinite-fGn
samples. At $\theta=0$ the power-law spectrum is flat on nonzero modes, but
DC removal still gives a finite-record anticorrelation. The truncated fGn
spectrum adds a small approximation. We use \emph{fGn-spectrum filter} with
these limitations throughout.

The assembled field has shape $(\mathrm{run},t,x)$: one filtered sequence
occupies the complete time column of each site. A memory reshape does not
exchange the physical site and time axes. New spectral archives carry an
explicit corrected-layout version. Earlier unversioned spectral archives are
excluded from all results below.

\section{Validation and statistical protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
\subsection{Validation of the production field}
For a centred jointly Gaussian pair with normalised covariance $\rho$, the
centred sign field $s=2\ders-1$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 C_s(\tau)=\langle s(t+\tau)s(t)\rangle=\frac2\pi\arcsin\rho(\tau).
 \label{eq:arcsin}
\end{equation}
The finite-period target $\rho$ is computed by inverse Fourier transforming
the actual sampled spectrum, including DC removal and alias truncation. It
differs from the infinite-fGn target. Equation~\eqref{eq:arcsin} is not imposed
as an identity on the uniform-driven AR input.

The production interface is tested at $\theta=0.25,0.40$, $L=32,64$ and
$T=65536$, with eight independent runs per filter and parameter pair.
Temporal products are circular to match the finite-period target; spatial
products test independence at nonzero lags. Errors are standard errors across
the eight run estimates. Predeclared numerical tolerances are five run standard
errors plus $0.003$ per reported correlation. These are engineering checks,
not simultaneous confidence statements. Regression tests also cover chunk
boundaries, repeated seeds and archive-version preservation.

For a negative control, the same correctly generated field is transposed to
$(\mathrm{run},x,t)$ and reshaped back to $(\mathrm{run},T,L)$ without the
proper axis mapping. This reproduces a layout fault identified during the
implementation audit. It retains filtered sequences but assigns correlations
to the wrong coordinates. No production result below uses this control.

\subsection{Simulation grid and late-time width}
Every input uses 64 independent runs at each combination of
\begin{align}
 L&=32,64,128,256,512,\\
 \theta&=0,0.10,1/6,0.20,0.25,0.30,0.40.
\end{align}
There are 105 cells and 6720 runs. The AR duration is
$T=\operatorname{round}(15L^{3/2})$; spectral durations round this upwards to a
5-smooth FFT length. At $L=32$ these are $2715$ and $2880$ sweeps, respectively;
at $L=512$ they are $173779$ and $174960$. Thus run counts, sizes and nominal
duration factors are matched, with exact durations retained in each archive.
Distinct runs and sites have independent streams. The two spectral filters
share Gaussian base streams within a parameter cell, so their agreement is
not treated as statistically independent replication.

Each run stores 32 geometric-time profiles and a regularly sampled width series
$W_r^2(t)=L^{-1}\sum_x[h_r(x,t)-\bar h_r(t)]^2$. We average squared widths
within each run over the last 20\% of stored width samples and then over runs,
\begin{equation}
 \overline W(L;\theta,T)=\left[\frac1R\sum_{r=1}^R
       \frac1{|\mathcal T|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}W_r^2(t)\right]^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:width}
\end{equation}
The width-sampling stride is $\max(1,\lfloor T/20000\rfloor)$ sweeps;
geometric sampling applies only to profile snapshots. If $n_W$ widths are
stored, the terminal mean uses $m=\max(1,\lfloor0.2n_W\rfloor)$ samples,
and the preceding mean uses the previous $m$ samples. Thus these windows
approximate the final and preceding fifths of elapsed time, up to stride and
integer rounding. For example, the fGn-spectrum cell at $L=256$ uses sweeps
$49155$--$61440$ for its terminal window, with a three-sweep stride.
The five-size slope of $\ln\overline W$ against $\ln L$ is called
$\alpha_{\mathrm{fit}}$. The adjacent-size slope is
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_{\mathrm{eff}}(L,2L)=
 \frac{\ln[\overline W(2L)/\overline W(L)]}{\ln2}.
 \label{eq:pair}
\end{equation}
Neither is assumed asymptotic. We compare the final 20\% time window with
the preceding 20\% as a finite-time diagnostic rather than declaring saturation
from duration alone. Widths, flat-pair fractions, window ratios and size slopes
use bootstrap standard deviations from 500 resamples of independent runs.
Whole run records remain together; different sizes are resampled independently.
All adjacent-size estimates use the same resulting bootstrap draws. Five-point
fit residuals are not substituted for run-to-run uncertainty.

\subsection{Local distances and sampling uncertainty}
We compare exact increment pmfs without rescaling their support or introducing
histogram bins. The Hellinger distance is
\cite{Hellinger1909,Bhattacharyya1943}
\begin{equation}
 H(P,Q)=\left[1-\sum_j\sqrt{P_jQ_j}\right]^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:H}
\end{equation}
For Eq.~\eqref{eq:pmf},
\begin{equation}
 H^2=1-\sqrt{p_\theta p_0}-\sqrt{(1-p_\theta)(1-p_0)}.
 \label{eq:localH}
\end{equation}
It contains only the flat-pair fraction and does not measure spatial
arrangements or establish a universality class.
For $p_\theta=p_0+\delta p$ with $0<p_0<1$, expansion gives
\begin{equation}
 H^2=\frac{(\delta p)^2}{8p_0(1-p_0)}+O((\delta p)^3).
 \label{eq:Hexpansion}
\end{equation}
This relation explains the local probability sensitivity of the distance;
the distance is not an independent spatial morphology observable.

At each size and for each input, the zero-parameter ensemble is the reference.
Equal halves of independent runs give the signal comparison; two disjoint
reference halves give its null. The excess is
\begin{equation}
 \Delta H=\langle H(P_{\theta,\mathrm{half}},P_{0,a})\rangle
             -\langle H(P_{0,a},P_{0,b})\rangle,
 \label{eq:excess}
\end{equation}
averaged over 120 partitions. An outer bootstrap of 200 independent-run
resamples repeats this complete estimator, preserving the shared reference
within each resample. Its standard deviation supplies the errors. The
standard deviation across conditional null partitions is a separate quantity.
We define $\Delta H(0,L)=0$ as the reference point instead of treating a
self-comparison as an independent signal experiment.

We do not infer a physical onset from the first significance crossing. A
shrinking null floor can shift a crossing even when local probabilities are
size independent. Conversely, local probabilities can remain fixed while
spatial arrangements reorganise. Appendix~\ref{app:local} supplies a direct
counterexample. No physical conclusion here relies on a novelty detector or
nominal distance $z$ scores.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Spatial and temporal input checks}
All eight selected spectral validation cells satisfy the stated checks at the
reported lags. At $\theta=0.25$, $L=32$, the fGn-spectrum field has binary
lag-one temporal correlation $\NoiseTimeMeasured$ against finite-period target
$\NoiseTimeTarget$, with spatial lag-one correlation consistent with zero.
The same samples under axis permutation instead give temporal correlation
$\NoiseLegacyTime$ and spatial correlation $\NoiseLegacySpace$.
Figure~\ref{fig:noise}(a,b) illustrates why testing the assembled field is
necessary even after validating the one-dimensional filter.

Innovation covariance also matters. At nominal $\theta=0$, the stationary
$N=48$ AR sums have $C(1)/C(0)=0.377315$ with shared innovations and $0.237958$
with independent innovations. For the shared case, $\tau C(\tau)/C(0)$ rises
from $1.04687$ at $\tau=64$ to $1.72174$ at $4096$; a constant-amplitude
$1/\tau$ tail does not describe that window. These raw-field observations do
not assign an RG phase to the binary growth model.

\subsection{Increasing finite-size width slopes}
The white-noise references give $\alpha_{\mathrm{fit}}=\AlphaSharedZero$,
$\AlphaPowerZero$ and $\AlphaFgnZero$ for shared AR, power law and the
fGn-spectrum input, close to $1/2$ over this window. The width slopes rise
with $\theta$ in all three campaigns (Fig.~\ref{fig:widths},
Table~\ref{tab:alpha}). Spatially independent spectral inputs do not produce
an approximately flat $1/2$ branch over this finite-size range.

At $\theta=0.25$, shared AR and the fGn-spectrum filter give
$\AlphaSharedQuarter$ and $\AlphaFgnQuarter$; at $0.40$ they give
$\AlphaSharedStrong$ and $\AlphaFgnStrong$. Thus construction changes
finite-size amplitudes and slopes, but the large rise with temporal correlation
also occurs under both Gaussian inputs. The maximum difference between the two
spectral-filter slopes is $\AlphaFilterMaxDiff$. Their base streams are
matched, and their agreement is not independent confirmation.

Adjacent-size estimates fluctuate rather than show a monotone flow. At
$\theta=0.25$ the fGn-spectrum sequence is approximately $0.681$, $0.645$,
$0.743$, $0.626$, with standard deviations about $0.030$--$0.035$.
This does not establish convergence to $1/2$ or a long-range fixed point.
Across all cells, the final-to-preceding-window RMS width ratio lies between
$\LateRatioMin$ and $\LateRatioMax$. This checks late-time stability without
proving that every finite-time correction has vanished; duration, cutoff and
initialisation remain part of the protocol.

\subsection{Paired filter comparison and duration controls}
\label{sec:controls}
For the Gaussian filters, corresponding run records are paired by seed at
each size and parameter. We resample the 64 pairs jointly within each cell,
independently between sizes, and refit the difference
$\delta\alpha=\alpha_{\mathrm{fGn}}-\alpha_{\mathrm{power}}$ in each
of 2000 bootstrap replicates. Pointwise 95\% percentile intervals contain
zero at all seven parameters (Table~\ref{tab:paired}). At $\theta=0.25$,
$\delta\alpha=-0.00443$ with interval $[-0.01104,0.00219]$.
Thus the present ensembles do not resolve a difference between these width
slopes; this is neither an equivalence test with a prespecified margin nor
independent replication of a scaling law.

they do not establish convergence with respect to the spectral cutoff.

\subsection{Local morphology and detection floors}
At $L=256$, the flat-pair fraction decreases as correlations strengthen for
every input [Fig.~\ref{fig:local}(a)]. For the fGn-spectrum filter it changes
from $\PflatFgnZero$ at $\theta=0$ to $\PflatFgnStrong$ at $0.40$; shared AR
changes from $\PflatSharedZero$ to $\PflatSharedStrong$. There is no
opposite-sign response between the correctly assembled inputs on this grid.
At $\theta=0.40$, $L=256$, the shared-AR and fGn-spectrum late-time widths are
$\WidthSharedStrong$ and $\WidthFgnStrong$, a ratio of $\WidthStrongRatio$.
Figure~\ref{fig:morphology} compares their final profiles and ensemble spatial
spectra. A single size and spectrum are not used to identify a faceting or
anomalous-scaling boundary.

The Hellinger excess detects local changes under correlated driving
[Fig.~\ref{fig:local}(b)], but its magnitude and size dependence do not define
a unique physical onset. In the fGn-spectrum reference, the conditional null
mean changes from $\NullFloorSmall$ at $L=32$ to $\NullFloorLarge$ at $512$,
a factor of $\NullFloorRatio$, despite matched run counts. Intermediate sizes
need not be perfectly monotone. The number of columns and their spatial
correlations affect each run's pooled-pmf precision.

A first crossing of a fixed number of null standard deviations mixes local
probability changes with detection power. This statement concerns the estimator
used here. Without reconstructing another study's observable, samples and
decision rule, it does not establish that a reported threshold has the same
origin. A detectable local difference likewise does not demonstrate a change
of scaling universality.

\section{Discussion}
The central result is a finite-window comparison in which input validation
and statistical comparison are treated separately. Both Gaussian spectral
filters yield increasing width slopes with temporal memory, and paired
resampling does not resolve their slope differences at the tested parameters.
The shared AR variant differs most visibly at weak and intermediate memory.
Agreement between the Gaussian filters therefore does not imply insensitivity
to every noise construction, while their common trend shows that increasing
width slopes are not exclusive to shared AR innovations.

The validation controls identify two distinct requirements. First, correlation
measurements must reach the final space--time field passed to the growth rule.
An axis-assignment fault can preserve filtered sequences while altering the
coordinates in which their correlations act. Second, an AR specification must
include the covariance of its innovations across modes. Identical mode weights
with shared and independent innovations describe different input processes.
Our analytic comparison establishes this distinction; isolating its effect on
growth would require a separate matched innovation-control campaign.

The fixed-record controls address observation duration without changing the
lowest available input frequency within each cell. Their interpretation is
correspondingly restricted to that input record and to the tested sizes and
parameters. They do not control the difference between periodic spectral
inputs and cold-started AR streams. Residual differences can also involve
short-time correlations and non-Gaussian marginals. Matching nominal $\theta$
is insufficient to isolate any of these mechanisms.

Local distances answer a different question from spatial scaling.
Equation~\eqref{eq:localH} and its expansion show that the increment-pmf
Hellinger distance measures changes in one local probability. Its sampling
floor affects detection power, but the distance discards the arrangement of
steps. The explicit counterexample in Appendix~\ref{app:local} shows why
neither a constant distance nor a detectable departure identifies a scaling
universality class. Comparisons with emergence diagnostics
\cite{WangXia2025} must retain the observable and sampling rule of each study.

Microscopic sensitivity under temporal correlations has already been
emphasised \cite{RodriguezFernandez2024}. The present work adds an explicit
field-assembly control, innovation-covariance specification, paired filter
inference and fixed-record duration checks in one discrete model. The
production grid stops at $L=512$ and uses durations proportional to $L^{3/2}$;
bootstrap errors do not quantify residual cutoff or initialization effects.
Establishing asymptotic exponents or a faceting boundary would require larger
sizes, independently controlled cutoffs and stationary input comparisons.
The different threshold definitions in the literature therefore remain
separate physical questions, and no historical threshold is invalidated here.

\section{Conclusion}
Specifying the complete noise process and observation protocol makes the
finite-window comparison interpretable. Correctly assembled Gaussian spectral
inputs and the shared AR variant all show increasing width slopes and
decreasing flat-pair fractions as temporal correlations strengthen. Paired
run-bootstrap inference resolves the uncertainty in the Gaussian-filter
comparison, and fixed-record controls test duration sensitivity separately
from a changing spectral cutoff. Exact innovation covariances and the local-pmf
reduction explain why generator labels and local distance thresholds cannot
substitute for these checks. The resulting benchmark supports finite-window
comparisons without identifying them with asymptotic exponents or a measured
critical temporal parameter.

\section{Single-step constraint}
\label{app:single}
If all neighbour differences have magnitude at most one, Eq.~\eqref{eq:bd}
gives $h(x,t)\le h(x,t+1)\le h(x,t)+1$. The maximum at the next site includes
$h(x,t)$, so $h(x+1,t+1)\ge h(x,t)$ and
$h(x,t+1)-h(x+1,t+1)\le1$. Exchanging sites gives the reverse bound.
Induction from the flat state proves the constraint. Periodic summation forces
equal up- and down-step counts. No height-inversion symmetry of the maximum
update is needed.

\section{Covariance and limiting behaviour}
\label{app:cov}
Stationarity gives $\Sigma_{nm}=r_nr_m\Sigma_{nm}+Q_{nm}$, hence
$\Sigma_{nm}=Q_{nm}/(1-r_nr_m)$ and Eq.~\eqref{eq:arcov}. With zero initial
modes and $t$ completed innovations, the covariance instead is
\begin{equation}
 C_N(t,\tau)=\sum_{n,m}W_nW_mr_n^\tau Q_{nm}
              \frac{1-(r_nr_m)^t}{1-r_nr_m}.
\end{equation}
A finite cold-start record is not silently identified with a stationary one.
The stationary shared-innovation spectrum is
\begin{equation}
 S_N^{\mathrm{shared}}(\omega)=\sigma_z^2
       \left|\sum_n\frac{W_n}{1-r_ne^{-i\omega}}\right|^2,
\end{equation}
whereas independent innovations give a sum of squared transfer functions.
Cross terms cannot be discarded in the shared case.

The impulse response $g_j=\sum_nW_nr_n^j$ has continuum-grid approximation
$g_j\propto j^{\theta-1}$ for intermediate $u_Nj\ll1\ll u_1j$. At
$\theta=0$, a $1/j$ kernel combined with
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac1{j(j+\tau)}=
 \frac{H_\tau}{\tau},\qquad H_\tau\simeq\ln\tau+\gamma,
\end{equation}
permits a logarithmic correction. This mechanism argument is not an exact
finite-$N$ pure-power asymptote: every finite-mode covariance ultimately
decays exponentially. Convergence at fixed lags does not exchange infinite-mode
and infinite-lag limits.

\section{Information missing from a local pmf}
\label{app:local}
On an even periodic lattice, consider equal counts of $+1$ and $-1$ increments
without zeros. Alternating signs and a block of $L/2$ positive followed by
$L/2$ negative increments have identical nearest-neighbour pmfs. Yet the
alternating interface has bounded width and the block arrangement has width
proportional to $L$. These are information-theoretic examples, not assertions
about stationary states of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bd}. They show why the single-step
constraint does not make a local pmf sufficient for large-scale roughness.
Constant $\Delta H$ cannot rule out a crossover, and increasing $\Delta H$
cannot alone establish one.
